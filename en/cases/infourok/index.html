Context

At the time of the project, Infourok was already one of the largest players in the Russian education market. But its model was changing: the product had to grow from a library of educational content into a marketplace and a social ecosystem for creators and learners

The client came to aic for a comprehensive refresh of its brand and digital products. The visual update was only part of the task

Business model

  • Commission on sales of materials and courses
  • Paid content promotion by creators
  • Infourok's own courses
  • Advertising

The key shift

content library → educational platform → marketplace + social ecosystem

Business challenge

The product had grown, but its interface and visual language no longer matched its scale and new strategy. The platform had to do more than help people consume content: it had to create conditions in which creators come to publish and sell educational products, while the platform earns a commission

BeforeAfter
A library of materials and a course catalogAn educational content marketplace
A simple registration formPersonalized onboarding
Limited user accountsA full creator / seller experience and a learning dashboard
An outdated visual languageA new brand, architecture, UX and UI

The main goal

create a modern product shell that supports the new business model and scales to future digital products.

Goals and metrics

I don't have final post-launch figures: I left the project before the measurements were completed. So below are the goals and metrics the product was aiming for, not results

Business goalTarget product metrics
More course and material salesCourse purchase conversion, Revenue / GMV
More active creatorsNew creator registrations, Creator activation
More engagement and return visitsContent engagement, DAU / retention
More completed learningCourse completion rate, Learning activity

Every change was tied to a specific user action: find → evaluate → buy → complete the course, or create → publish → promote → sell

Audience and ecosystem

Learners / consumers

Teachers, school students, parents, tutors, curriculum specialists, psychologists, university lecturers, college students and other consumers of educational content. Journey: discovery → evaluation → purchase → learning → completion

Creators / sellers

Teachers, curriculum specialists, tutors, psychologists, university lecturers and other experts who create, publish and sell educational content. Journey: create → publish → promote → analyze → earn

The core product conflict

a single platform has to both quickly lead users to the right learning and give creators a full seller experience.

Research and insights

Most of the discovery was run by Infourok's product team: interviews, surveys, Yandex Metrica analysis, user typologies, feedback collection and reviews with educational content experts.

I wasn't the lead researcher. My work started with interpreting the findings and turning them into UX decisions: IA, CJM, user flows, prototypes and UI.

InsightWhat it meant for the product
The product knows almost nothing about the userPersonalization should start as early as onboarding
A huge amount of diverse contentClear information architecture and discovery are needed
A creator is not just a userA dedicated creator / seller experience is needed
Learning is a process, not a purchaseThe dashboard should show progress and the next step

Takeaway

Infourok sits at the intersection of a learning platform, a content library, a social layer and a marketplace. Copying the UX of a single competitor wasn't an option.

#1. Onboarding and personalization

From simple registration to a personalized learning experience.

AS IS

A standard registration form with personal details. Users created an account, but the product knew almost nothing about their educational context and couldn't immediately offer relevant content.

TO BE

Registration + personalization: role, field, subjects and interests, specialists of interest, learning goal and expected outcome – for example, a certificate.

Hypothesis

if we understand the user's educational context during onboarding, we can build a more relevant starting feed and recommendations.

Flow: registration → role → interests → subjects → specialists → goal → personal feed

#2. Course, event and material catalogs

Three content types – one framework for search and selection.

Courses, events and materials had historically lived in separate catalogs, each with its own logic. With millions of content items, users need to narrow down results quickly, and the platform needs to show equally clear cards that are easy to compare and decide on.

Solution

a single framework for all three catalogs – search with popular queries, curated collections, sorting and filters in the sidebar. Only the card changes: it shows what matters for each specific content type.

What's shared

Search and collections

  • Catalog search and popular queries
  • Themed collections ("Pick of the day")
  • Sorting: popular, by date, price low / high, by rating

Filters

  • Field, type, area, discipline, format
  • Search within long lists of areas
  • "N more areas / filters" – long lists expand on demand

What changes: a card for each content type

CatalogWhat the card showsPrimary action
CoursesProgram type (professional development / retraining / self-development), hours, rating and reviews, how many are studying and have completed it, price and discountApply
EventsFormat (webinar, seminar, masterclass, club), date or live stream, recording length, authorWatch / join
MaterialsFile format (PDF, DOCX, PPTX), author, rating, views and downloadsDownload

Mobile version

On mobile, filters and sorting move into separate full-screen layers: the results stay clean, and complex filtering doesn't break scrolling.

#3. Course page and master page

Not a single beautiful landing page, but a builder that assembles a page for any course.

AS IS

The old course page was a long landing page: blocks were repeated (the "Start learning now" call to action appeared twice), the benefits took up several screens, and the essentials – dates, certificate, price – got lost. The page for each program area was essentially assembled by hand.

Solution: the master page

I built a master page – a library of ~28 modular blocks with a single grid and set of rules. Each block answers one user question.

Block groupWhat it includesQuestion it answers
Key informationHero: course type, title, rating, number of learners; quick facts – start date, format, certificate, duration, price and installmentsWhat is it, and is it right for me?
ContentDescription, curriculum, study plan, about the profession, who it's for, who can enrollWhat will I learn?
TrustReviews from external platforms, license, Skolkovo, instructors, graduate storiesWhy can I trust this platform?
OutcomeThe certificate issued, with a sample, and graduate statisticsWhat will I get at the end?
Purchase and supportPrice and why it's low, discounted application, Premium subscription, how learning works, 24/7 support, similar coursesHow do I start, and what if I have questions?

One system – different goals and color schemes

The master page is used to assemble pages for different product types. What changes is the set and order of blocks, the accent color and the certificate block – depending on what matters most for the purchase decision.

TypeUser's goalAccentWhat comes first
Professional developmentQuickly get a certificate needed for work● green + ● orangeStart date, accelerated format, certificate, curriculum
Professional retrainingChange careers – takes longer to decide, higher price● blueTrust: reviews, license, platform rating, instructors, about the profession
Mini-courseTry out a field with a small investment● purpleDescription, career benefits, curriculum; a certificate instead of a diploma

The "Certificate issued" block is an example of a component that adapts to the program type: a retraining diploma, a professional development certificate, or a mini-course certificate.

What it gave the product

a new course page is assembled from ready-made blocks with no separate design work, and color-coding by program type helps users instantly recognize the learning format.

#4. Course application in the user account

From scattered forms to one clear scenario.

AS IS

In the version we started from, the application was split across several states and screens: "access closed", card payment right in the course list, personal details, documents and delivery address – all in different places. Users couldn't tell what had already been done and what was still needed to receive their certificate.

TO BE

The application became a single page with sequential steps. On the right are the application status and a personal advisor users can contact at any time.

  • General information – training center and course schedule
  • Learning – duration, module progress, link to lessons
  • Certificate delivery – ordering and address
  • Personal details – auto-filled from the profile, just need checking
  • Documents – upload passport, diploma and other scans, submit for review
  • Payment – self-funded or via the Premium subscription

Three application states

StateWhat the user seesNext step
Application not paidChoice of hours, details form, payment blockPay or subscribe
Application paid, delivery not paidLearning progress, an offer to order a printed certificateStudy and order delivery
Application and delivery paidProgress, delivery address, completed details and documentsComplete the modules and the final assessment

Principle

at any stage, users see where they are and what to do next, and the system pre-fills everything it already knows about them.

Design System

There was no unified design system at the start. Once the brand language was approved, we built the UI foundation from scratch.

Brand → tokens → UI Kit → components → patterns → product

What it included

  • Tokens, colors, typography
  • Components and their states
  • Patterns and documentation

My role

  • Planning the UI Kit structure
  • Building the foundation: tokens and base components
  • Delegating tasks to two middle designers, reviewing their work and keeping it consistent

Validation

Key scenarios went through a cycle: problem → hypotheses → options → prototype → testing → final UI.

What we managed to do: hallway testing, internal UX reviews, expert interviews and surveys with Infourok's team of content specialists.

What we didn't get to

Full A/B tests and long-term product analytics continued after I left the project, so I don't use their results in this case study.

Stakeholders and development

Stakeholder management

Infourok had a strong product team but no in-house design ownership, and on a number of issues the final say rested with a leader who had his own vision. Sometimes the team supported a decision and the leader didn't.

  • Argued for decisions through the user's task and the business goal
  • Presented several options rather than defending a single design
  • Proposed testing instead of debating taste
  • Once a business decision was made, adapted the design without losing the core UX logic

Development and handoff

The client had in-house developers and contractors. I worked directly with the frontend and backend leads, discussing feasibility, constraints and the documentation format – component states, responsive rules, annotations, edge cases.

Process: design → design review → development discussion → handoff → design grooming → implementation

After I left, the team continued to receive design support from aic during implementation.

What I would do differently

01

Research earlier

I would bring research in at an earlier stage – not only to understand users, but also as an argument in contested decisions.

02

Stakeholder alignment

I would agree on the criteria upfront: what the product team decides, where executive approval is needed, and what gets confirmed by testing.

03

Test before debate

When the team and a stakeholder see a solution differently, it's better to agree in advance on how to test it than to argue about taste.
